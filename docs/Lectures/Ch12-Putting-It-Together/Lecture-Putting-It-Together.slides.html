Each design rests on a different assumption. When designs with different weaknesses agree, the conclusion is much harder to dismiss.

Experimental and Observational Estimates

Checking Designs Against Each Other

  • LaLonde’s benchmark: standard non-experimental estimates of a job-training program often missed the experimental answer (LaLonde 1986)
  • Within one paper: Cui et al. report both DiD and experimental (IV) estimates for each firm. The DiD estimates are sometimes larger and sometimes smaller: the direction of selection differs across firms
  • Across papers: an AI assistant raised support-agent productivity by about 15% in a staggered rollout (Brynjolfsson et al.), and a coding assistant raised completed tasks by about 26% in randomized experiments (Cui et al.). Different settings and estimands, but the same direction and similar size

Lesson: observational designs are most credible when their assumptions can be checked against an experiment, or against a design with different assumptions.

Accumulating Evidence

Combining Estimates Across Studies

Precision Weighting

Given estimates \hat\tau_1, \dots, \hat\tau_K with standard errors s_1, \dots, s_K of the same estimand:

\hat\tau_{\text{pooled}} = \frac{\sum_k w_k \hat\tau_k}{\sum_k w_k}, \qquad w_k = \frac{1}{s_k^2}, \qquad SE(\hat\tau_{\text{pooled}}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_k w_k}}

  • Precise studies get more weight
  • This is how Cui et al. combine their three noisy experiments into one informative estimate
  • If true effects differ across settings, a random-effects model adds a between-study variance \tau^2, which widens the pooled interval and shifts weight towards smaller studies

Pooling in R

Code 
# Three hypothetical experiments estimating the same effect (in %)
est <- c(firm_A = 22, firm_B = 41, firm_C = 18)
se  <- c(firm_A = 12, firm_B = 25, firm_C = 15)

w <- 1 / se^2
pooled    <- sum(w * est) / sum(w)
pooled_se <- 1 / sqrt(sum(w))

# Test for heterogeneity across studies (Cochran's Q)
Q <- sum(w * (est - pooled)^2)

round(c(pooled = pooled, se = pooled_se,
        lower_95 = pooled - 1.96 * pooled_se, upper_95 = pooled + 1.96 * pooled_se,
        Q = Q, p_heterogeneity = pchisq(Q, df = length(est) - 1, lower.tail = FALSE)), 2)
         pooled              se        lower_95        upper_95               Q 
          22.97            8.77            5.77           40.17            0.64 
p_heterogeneity 
           0.73
  • None of the three hypothetical experiments is significant on its own at the 5% level, but the pooled estimate is.
  • The heterogeneity test has little power with three studies; a non-significant Q does not prove effects are identical.

What Goes Wrong at the Level of a Literature

Selective Reporting and Publication Bias

  • If studies with significant results are more likely to be published, the published literature overstates effects, even if every individual study is unbiased
  • Small studies that happen to find large effects are especially likely to be published
  • Symptoms: many estimates just past the significance threshold; small studies reporting larger effects (an asymmetric “funnel plot”)

Remedies: pre-registration (Chapter 4); registered reports; publishing null results; replication packages with data and code; reporting failed experiments (Cui et al. report the abandoned Accenture experiment).

From Evidence to Policy

External Validity

Will It Work Here?

Questions to ask before applying a result elsewhere:

  • Population: are the people or firms similar? Compliers in an IV or experiment may differ from the target population
  • Setting: institutions, prices, and alternatives differ across countries and sectors
  • Scale: effects in a pilot may change when everyone is treated (market prices adjust; supply of trainers runs out; others respond)
  • Time: technologies change. An AI tool from 2021 is not the tool of 2026
  • Mechanism: if we know why something works, we can judge whether that reason holds elsewhere

Synthesis Example: Generative AI at Work

What Four Studies, Four Designs, Say Together

Study Design Finding
Cui et al. (2026) Field experiments (Ch. 4–5) Coding assistant raises completed tasks ~26%; larger gains and adoption for newer developers
Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) Staggered rollout, FE/DiD (Ch. 8–9) Support productivity +15%; +30% for less skilled and less experienced agents; small gains for the most experienced
Demirci et al. (2025) DiD on ChatGPT’s release (Ch. 9) Freelance job posts in writing and coding fall ~21% relative to manual work
Shaw and Nave (2026) (preprint) Lab and online experiments (Ch. 4) When they consult the AI, people follow its wrong answers ~80% of the time (Study 1)

Consistent pattern: within firms, AI complements less experienced workers; across markets, it substitutes for some outsourced tasks; and people over-rely on its output. A decision-maker needs all three.

Reporting an Evaluation

A Checklist for the Write-Up

  1. Question and estimand: the effect of what, on what, for whom
  2. Design: where the counterfactual comes from, stated in plain language
  3. Assumptions and the evidence for them (balance, first stage, pre-trends, placebo tests)
  4. Main estimate with uncertainty, in units a decision-maker understands
  5. Robustness to alternative specifications and estimators
  6. Heterogeneity: who gains and who loses
  7. Limitations: what the design cannot rule out; external validity
  8. Costs and benefits where possible
  9. Replication materials: data access, code, pre-registration

Communicating to Decision-Makers

Translate Estimates into Decisions

  • Use meaningful units: “one fewer death for every 1,587 people sent a letter” (Goldin et al.); “about 15,600 deaths averted had all states expanded Medicaid” (Miller et al.)
  • Report uncertainty: a range as well as a point estimate
  • Say whose effect it is (compliers, treated units, units at a cutoff)
  • Separate what the evidence shows from what you recommend
  • Be explicit about what is not known

Summary

Summary

  • Every method in these notes finds a counterfactual in a different place, and rests on a different assumption
  • Strong studies combine designs whose weaknesses differ; strong conclusions rest on several studies
  • Pool comparable estimates with precision weights; check for heterogeneity
  • Guard against publication bias with pre-registration, replication, and reporting null and failed results
  • External validity needs as much attention as internal validity
  • Report evaluations so that a decision-maker can see the estimand, the assumptions, the uncertainty, and the limits
