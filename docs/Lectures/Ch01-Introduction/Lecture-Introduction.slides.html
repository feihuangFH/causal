Introduction to Policy Evaluation and Causal Inference

Why Every Evaluation Needs a Counterfactual

Fei Huang, UNSW Sydney
  • This chapter sets up the rest of the notes: what policy evaluation is, why it is hard, and how each method in these notes solves the same core problem in a different way.
  • The running example is health insurance and mortality, a question where naive comparisons give the wrong sign.
  • We finish with a roadmap of the methods and how to choose between them.

Why Policy Evaluation

Policies Rest on Causal Claims

Every Policy Embeds a Causal Claim

  • Expanding public health insurance will reduce deaths among low-income adults
  • Giving workers a generative AI assistant will raise productivity
  • Subsidizing insurance premiums will increase take-up
  • Higher credit limits will increase borrowing and stimulate spending

Each claim is about what would happen with the policy compared with what would happen without it.

What Policy Evaluation Asks

  1. Did it work? Is there an effect at all?
  2. How much? Is the effect large enough to matter relative to cost?
  3. For whom? Who gains, who loses, and who is unaffected?
  4. Why? Through which mechanism?
  5. Would it work elsewhere? At a larger scale, in another country, for another population?

Three Kinds of Questions

Data Can Answer Different Questions

Type Example (insurance) What it needs
Description What share of adults have no health insurance? Good measurement and sampling
Prediction Which policyholders are likely to lodge a claim next year? Patterns that hold in new data
Causation Would a premium subsidy make uninsured people buy cover? A counterfactual: what happens with vs. without the subsidy

Prediction Is Not Causation

A model can predict claims very well using variables that do not cause claims (postcode, car colour, the time of day a quote is requested). Machine learning models and LLMs are excellent prediction engines. A policy question, however, asks what would happen if we changed something. That is a causal question, and good prediction does not answer it.

These notes are about causal questions.

The Fundamental Problem

Potential Outcomes

Two Outcomes, Only One Observed

For each person i and a treatment D_i \in \{0, 1\} (e.g., having health insurance):

  • Y_i(1): the outcome if person i is treated
  • Y_i(0): the outcome if person i is not treated
  • The causal effect for person i is Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)

We observe only one of the two: Y_i = D_i \, Y_i(1) + (1 - D_i) \, Y_i(0).

The other one, the counterfactual, is missing for everyone. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference (Holland 1986).

The Way Forward: Averages

We cannot learn individual effects, but we can learn average effects, such as the average treatment effect (ATE) E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)], if we can find a group whose outcomes stand in for the missing counterfactual. Every method in these notes is a different way of finding that group. Chapter 2 develops the framework formally.

Estimand, Estimator, Estimate

Three Words Worth Keeping Apart

  • Estimand: the quantity we want to know, defined with potential outcomes. Example: the average effect of being sent a letter encouraging insurance enrollment on two-year mortality, among previously uninsured adults aged 45–64: E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)] in that population, where treatment is receiving the letter
  • Estimator: the recipe we apply to data. Example: the difference in mean two-year mortality between people randomly sent and not sent the letter
  • Estimate: the number the recipe produces in our sample. Example: a fall of about 0.06 percentage points (Chapter 3)

A design is credible when its estimator recovers the estimand under assumptions we can defend. A precise estimate of the wrong estimand is still wrong.

Why Naive Comparisons Fail

Comparing the Insured with the Uninsured

The naive comparison of mortality between insured and uninsured people decomposes into two terms:

\underbrace{E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - E[Y_i \mid D_i = 0]}_{\text{observed difference}} = \underbrace{E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1]}_{\text{effect on the treated}} + \underbrace{E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1] - E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 0]}_{\text{selection bias}}

Selection bias is the difference the two groups would have had even without insurance.

  • People who expect high medical costs are more likely to buy insurance (adverse selection): selection bias pushes towards insurance looking harmful
  • People with higher income and education are more likely to be insured and are healthier anyway: selection bias pushes towards insurance looking helpful

Without a design, we cannot know which force dominates, or by how much.

Simulation Design

Insurance and Mortality in Two Worlds

  • People: 100,000 adults, each with an underlying health risk. The researcher does not observe it.
  • Without insurance: a person’s annual chance of dying (in percent) is 1.5 + 0.8 × risk, so sicker people die more often.
  • With insurance: that chance falls by 0.3 percentage points, or less for the few people already close to zero. The true average effect is therefore about −0.29.
  • Observational world: the sicker a person is, the more likely they are to buy insurance (adverse selection).
  • Experimental world: a coin flip decides who is insured.

In each world we compare the death rates of the insured and the uninsured.

Simulation: Selection Bias Can Flip the Sign

Code 
set.seed(2026)
n <- 100000

# Underlying health risk (higher = sicker); unobserved by the researcher
risk <- rnorm(n)

# Potential outcomes: annual probability of death (in percent)
y0 <- pmax(0, 1.5 + 0.8 * risk)          # without insurance
y1 <- pmax(0, y0 - 0.3)                  # insurance lowers mortality by up to 0.3 pp

# Observational world: sicker people are more likely to buy insurance
d_obs <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(1.5 * risk))
naive <- mean(ifelse(d_obs == 1, y1, y0)[d_obs == 1]) -
         mean(ifelse(d_obs == 1, y1, y0)[d_obs == 0])

# Experimental world: a coin flip decides who is insured
d_rct <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.5)
y_rct <- ifelse(d_rct == 1, y1, y0)
rct <- mean(y_rct[d_rct == 1]) - mean(y_rct[d_rct == 0])

# The two terms from the previous slide
att <- mean((y1 - y0)[d_obs == 1])                          # effect on the insured
selection <- mean(y0[d_obs == 1]) - mean(y0[d_obs == 0])    # selection bias

round(c(true_ATE = mean(y1 - y0), naive_comparison = naive, ATT = att,
        selection_bias = selection, randomized = rct), 3)
        true_ATE naive_comparison              ATT   selection_bias 
          -0.286            0.536           -0.299            0.835 
      randomized 
          -0.287

Reading the Result

In the observational world the insured die more often, so insurance looks harmful (+0.54 percentage points). That gap is the effect on the insured (−0.30) plus selection bias (+0.83), because sicker people bought insurance. The coin flip balances risk across the two groups, and the comparison recovers the true effect (−0.29).

  • The naive comparison says insurance raises mortality, because the insured are sicker to begin with. Randomization removes the selection term and recovers the true protective effect.

Other Ways Correlations Mislead

Six Traps

Trap What it means Example
Confounding A third factor drives both the treatment and the outcome, so the two move together even if the treatment does nothing Larger, better-managed firms are more likely to adopt AI tools and are more productive anyway
Reverse causality The outcome, or what predicts it, causes the treatment Struggling workers are given AI tools because they are struggling, so AI users look less productive
Controlling for a mechanism Adjusting for something the treatment itself changes removes part of the effect you want to measure Insurance improves health partly through more doctor visits; holding visits fixed hides that channel
Reversion to the mean Units picked for an extreme value tend to move back towards average next time, with or without any intervention Drivers enrolled in a safety program after an unusually bad year file fewer claims the next year anyway
Selection into the sample The data contain only units that passed a filter related to the outcome Studying only firms that survived a crisis hides the ones the crisis destroyed
Searching and selective reporting Trying many analyses and reporting only the significant ones produces chance findings With 20 outcomes tested, about one will be “significant” at 5% by chance alone

Internal and External Validity

Two Kinds of Validity

  • Internal validity: is the estimate right for the people, place, and time studied?
  • External validity: does it carry over to other populations, places, or scales?

Strong designs often buy internal validity with a narrow setting (one firm, one state, units near a cutoff). Keep both in view.

The Evaluation Toolkit

Who Decided Who Was Treated?

The Question That Organizes These Notes

Every method finds a counterfactual by exploiting how treatment was assigned. The notes move from designs where the researcher controls assignment, through designs that exploit a known source of variation (a shock, a rule, timing), to designs that rely only on measured characteristics:

Who or what decided treatment? Where the counterfactual comes from Method Part · Ch.
The researcher A lottery Randomized experiment II · 3–5
An outside shock Units nudged into treatment by something unrelated to outcomes Instrumental variables III · 6
A rule Units just on the other side of the cutoff Regression discontinuity IV · 7
The units, observed over time The same units before treatment, or the change in untreated units Fixed effects, DiD, synthetic control V · 8–10
The units, observed once Similar units, compared on observed characteristics Regression, matching VI · 11

Choosing a Design

  • Walk through the five questions left to right. Each “no” moves to a design with less control over assignment; the final step relies on the strongest untestable assumption, that every confounder is measured.
  • This is a starting point, not a rule. Strong evaluations often combine designs (Chapter 12).

Moving Between Methods

When One Design Runs Into Trouble

Situation Move to Why
An experiment where not everyone takes up the treatment IV (Ch. 5–6) Random assignment is an instrument for take-up
A cutoff rule that is not followed exactly Fuzzy RDD = IV at the cutoff (Ch. 6–7) Crossing the cutoff shifts the probability of treatment
An instrument that is weak or hard to defend, but you have panel data Fixed effects or DiD (Ch. 8–9) Use changes within units instead
Treated and comparison groups differ in levels Fixed effects or DiD (Ch. 8–9) Fixed differences cancel out
Policy adopted at different times by different units Heterogeneity-robust DiD (Ch. 9) Static two-way fixed effects can mislead
Only one or a few treated units, no single good comparison Synthetic control (Ch. 10) Build a comparison from a weighted mix
Parallel trends plausible only for similar units DiD with matching or reweighting (Ch. 9, 11) Compare like with like, then difference

The Credibility Revolution

Design Before Estimation

Over the past three decades, empirical economics and management research shifted from “control for everything and hope” to research design: a clear source of variation in treatment that is plausibly as good as random (Angrist and Pischke 2010).

What that means in practice:

  • State the comparison that identifies the effect, in plain language
  • Make the identifying assumption explicit
  • Show evidence for it (balance, pre-trends, placebo tests, first stages)
  • Report robustness, and state what the design cannot rule out

Case Study: An AI Assistant in a Support Centre

The Setting

A Customer-Support Centre

A software firm runs a customer-support centre. Its agents resolve about two customer issues per hour. The firm wants to know whether a generative AI assistant raises that number.

The data on the next slides are simulated, so we know the true answer: the assistant adds 0.3 resolutions per hour for every agent.

Each slide tells a different story about who got the assistant. The question stays the same. The assignment changes, and so does the method that can answer the question.

In every story, a simple comparison gives the wrong answer. By a simple (or naive) comparison we mean the obvious one: agents with the tool against agents without it, or output after against output before, ignoring how the tool was assigned.

  • This is an illustrative case with simulated data. The method chapters use published studies; this one keeps the setting fixed so that only the assignment mechanism changes.
  • The setting echoes the generative AI study of customer-support agents in Chapters 8 and 9, but every number here is made up.

Design 1: Randomized Experiment

Managers give the assistant to the agents they expect to use it well, who tend to be their strongest. The firm could instead hold a lottery.

Code 
suppressMessages(library(fixest))
f2 <- function(x) sub("^-", "&minus;", sprintf("%.2f", x))   # format estimates
blue <- "#1565c0"; orange <- "#c2410c"; grey <- "grey55"
tau <- 0.3                                                # true effect

set.seed(11)
n <- 2000
skill <- rnorm(n)                                         # unobserved by the analyst
y0 <- 2.0 + 0.3 * skill + rnorm(n, 0, 0.3)                # resolutions per hour without AI
pick <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(1.5 * skill))                 # managers pick stronger agents
y_pick <- y0 + tau * pick
naive_pick <- mean(y_pick[pick == 1]) - mean(y_pick[pick == 0])

lottery <- sample(rep(0:1, n / 2))                        # the alternative: a lottery
y_rct <- y0 + tau * lottery
rct <- mean(y_rct[lottery == 1]) - mean(y_rct[lottery == 0])

par(mfrow = c(1, 2), mar = c(4, 4, 2, 1))
hist(skill[pick == 0], breaks = 30, col = adjustcolor(grey, 0.5), border = NA, xlim = c(-3.5, 3.5),
     main = "Skill of agents, by who got the tool", xlab = "Skill (unobserved)")
hist(skill[pick == 1], breaks = 30, col = adjustcolor(blue, 0.5), border = NA, add = TRUE)
legend("topleft", c("No tool", "Tool"), fill = adjustcolor(c(grey, blue), 0.5), border = NA, bty = "n")
barplot(c(Managers = naive_pick, Lottery = rct), col = c(grey, blue), border = NA, ylim = c(0, 0.7),
        main = "Estimated effect", ylab = "Resolutions per hour")
abline(h = tau, col = orange, lty = 2, lwd = 2)

Design 1: Randomized Experiment (continued)

How the Method Fits the Story

  • Simple comparison: agents with the tool against agents without it, when managers chose: 0.61. Too high, because the chosen agents were stronger to begin with (left panel).
  • Randomized experiment: a lottery decides who gets the tool, so both groups have the same mix of skill. Their difference is the effect: 0.31.
  • Relies on: assignment really being random, and treated agents not affecting untreated ones.

Design 2: Instrumental Variables

Use of the tool is voluntary. The firm emails a random half of agents encouraging them to try it. Motivated agents use it more, with or without the email.

Code 
set.seed(12)
n_iv <- 8000
email <- rbinom(n_iv, 1, 0.5)                             # randomized encouragement
motiv <- rnorm(n_iv)                                      # unobserved motivation
use <- rbinom(n_iv, 1, plogis(qlogis(0.2) + (qlogis(0.6) - qlogis(0.2)) * email + 1.2 * motiv))
y_iv <- 2.0 + 0.25 * motiv + rnorm(n_iv, 0, 0.3) + tau * use
first <- mean(use[email == 1]) - mean(use[email == 0])    # first stage
itt <- mean(y_iv[email == 1]) - mean(y_iv[email == 0])    # reduced form
as_treated <- mean(y_iv[use == 1]) - mean(y_iv[use == 0])
iv <- itt / first

par(mfrow = c(1, 2), mar = c(4, 4, 2, 1))
barplot(tapply(use, email, mean), names.arg = c("No email", "Email"), col = c(grey, blue), border = NA,
        ylim = c(0, 0.8), main = "Share using the tool", ylab = "Share")
barplot(tapply(y_iv, email, mean), names.arg = c("No email", "Email"), col = c(grey, blue), border = NA,
        ylim = c(1.8, 2.3), xpd = FALSE, main = "Resolutions per hour", ylab = "Mean")

Design 2: Instrumental Variables (continued)

How the Method Fits the Story

  • Simple comparison: agents who used the tool against those who did not: 0.51. Too high, because motivated agents both use it more and resolve more.
  • Instrumental variables: the email is random, so compare agents who got it with those who did not. It raised use by 0.33 and output by 0.10. Output rose only because use rose, so the effect of using the tool is 0.10 ÷ 0.33 = 0.31.
  • Relies on: the email changing output only through use of the tool.

Design 3: Regression Discontinuity

The firm gives the tool to agents who scored below 70 on a skills test.

Code 
set.seed(13)
score <- runif(n, 40, 100)
below <- as.integer(score < 70)                           # the rule
y_rd <- 1.0 + 0.015 * score + tau * below + rnorm(n, 0, 0.15)
naive_rd <- mean(y_rd[below == 1]) - mean(y_rd[below == 0])
near <- abs(score - 70) < 10                              # local linear fit within 10 points
rdd <- unname(coef(lm(y_rd ~ below * I(score - 70), subset = near))["below"])

bins <- cut(score, seq(40, 100, 2.5))
par(mar = c(4, 4, 1, 1))
plot(tapply(score, bins, mean), tapply(y_rd, bins, mean), pch = 19,
     col = ifelse(tapply(score, bins, mean) < 70, blue, grey),
     xlab = "Test score", ylab = "Resolutions per hour")
for (side in 0:1) {
  s <- if (side == 1) score < 70 & near else score >= 70 & near
  fit <- lm(y_rd[s] ~ score[s]); xs <- range(score[s])
  lines(xs, coef(fit)[1] + coef(fit)[2] * xs, lwd = 3, col = if (side == 1) blue else grey)
}
abline(v = 70, col = orange, lty = 2)

Design 3: Regression Discontinuity (continued)

How the Method Fits the Story

  • Simple comparison: everyone below 70 against everyone above: −0.14, the wrong sign, because low scorers are weaker agents.
  • Regression discontinuity: agents scoring 69 and 71 are almost equally skilled; only the tool differs. The jump in output at 70 is the effect: 0.33.
  • Relies on: nothing else changing at 70, and agents being unable to game their score.

Design 4: Panel Fixed Effects

Managers again give the tool to their strongest agents, at different months. We see every agent’s monthly output for a year.

Code 
set.seed(14)
na <- 300
sk <- rnorm(na)
gets <- rbinom(na, 1, plogis(1.5 * sk))                   # stronger agents get the tool
start <- ifelse(gets == 1, sample(4:9, na, replace = TRUE), Inf)
fe <- expand.grid(agent = 1:na, month = 1:12)
fe$tool <- as.integer(fe$month >= start[fe$agent])
fe$y <- 2.0 + 0.3 * sk[fe$agent] + tau * fe$tool + rnorm(nrow(fe), 0, 0.2)
pooled <- unname(coef(lm(y ~ tool, fe))["tool"])
fe_est <- unname(coef(feols(y ~ tool | agent + month, fe))["tool"])

treated <- which(gets == 1)[1:30]
before <- sapply(treated, function(a) mean(fe$y[fe$agent == a & fe$tool == 0]))
after <- sapply(treated, function(a) mean(fe$y[fe$agent == a & fe$tool == 1]))
par(mar = c(4, 4, 1, 1))
plot(NA, xlim = c(0.8, 2.2), ylim = range(before, after), xaxt = "n", xlab = "", ylab = "Resolutions per hour")
axis(1, at = 1:2, labels = c("Before the tool", "After the tool"))
segments(1, before, 2, after, col = adjustcolor(blue, 0.5), lwd = 2)
points(rep(1, 30), before, pch = 19, col = grey); points(rep(2, 30), after, pch = 19, col = blue)

Design 4: Panel Fixed Effects (continued)

How the Method Fits the Story

  • Simple comparison: months with the tool against months without, pooling all agents: 0.52. Too high, because the agents with the tool are the stronger ones.
  • Fixed effects: compare each agent with themselves before and after getting the tool (each line in the plot), so permanent skill differences cancel. The average change is 0.29.
  • Relies on: nothing else changing for an agent at the time they got the tool.

Design 5: Difference-in-Differences

Office A switches the tool on in month 7; office B never does. Output is rising in both offices for other reasons.

Code 
set.seed(15)
did <- expand.grid(office = c("A", "B"), month = 1:12, agent = 1:400)
did$tool <- as.integer(did$office == "A" & did$month >= 7)
did$y <- ifelse(did$office == "A", 2.0, 1.8) + 0.03 * did$month + tau * did$tool + rnorm(nrow(did), 0, 0.25)
m <- tapply(did$y, list(did$office, did$month >= 7), mean)
before_after <- m["A", "TRUE"] - m["A", "FALSE"]
did_est <- before_after - (m["B", "TRUE"] - m["B", "FALSE"])

path <- tapply(did$y, list(did$office, did$month), mean)
counterfactual <- path["A", 6] + (path["B", ] - path["B", 6])
par(mar = c(4, 4, 1, 1))
matplot(1:12, t(path), type = "l", lty = 1, lwd = 3, col = c(blue, grey), xlab = "Month", ylab = "Resolutions per hour")
lines(6:12, counterfactual[6:12], lty = 2, lwd = 2, col = blue)
abline(v = 6.5, col = orange, lty = 2)
legend("topleft", c("Office A", "Office B", "Office A without the tool (implied)"),
       col = c(blue, grey, blue), lty = c(1, 1, 2), lwd = c(3, 3, 2), bty = "n")

Design 5: Difference-in-Differences (continued)

How the Method Fits the Story

  • Simple comparison: office A after against before: 0.49. Too high, because it includes the rise that would have happened anyway.
  • Difference-in-differences: office B’s change over the same months (0.16) shows that rise. Subtracting it leaves the effect: 0.33. The dashed line is office A without the tool.
  • Relies on: office A following office B’s trend had it not adopted (parallel trends).

Design 6: Synthetic Control

Only the Manila office adopts, in month 13. Offices differ in how fast their output grows.

Code 
set.seed(16)
J <- 12; T0 <- 12; TT <- 24
load <- c(1.6, runif(J, 0.2, 2))                          # exposure to a growing trend; Manila first
trend <- seq(0, 1, length.out = TT)
Y <- t(sapply(1:(J + 1), function(i) 2.0 + rnorm(1, 0, 0.08) * (i > 1) + load[i] * trend + rnorm(TT, 0, 0.02)))
Y[1, (T0 + 1):TT] <- Y[1, (T0 + 1):TT] + tau               # Manila gets the tool in month 13

sc_weights <- function(y1, Y0) {                          # non-negative weights that sum to one
  loss <- function(a) { w <- exp(a) / sum(exp(a)); sum((y1 - colSums(w * Y0))^2) }
  a <- optim(rep(0, nrow(Y0)), loss, method = "BFGS", control = list(maxit = 2000))$par
  exp(a) / sum(exp(a))
}
w <- sc_weights(Y[1, 1:T0], Y[-1, 1:T0])
synth <- colSums(w * Y[-1, ])
post <- (T0 + 1):TT
sc_est <- mean(Y[1, post] - synth[post])
did_avg <- (mean(Y[1, post]) - mean(Y[1, 1:T0])) - (mean(colMeans(Y[-1, post])) - mean(colMeans(Y[-1, 1:T0])))

par(mar = c(4, 4, 1, 1))
plot(1:TT, Y[1, ], type = "l", lwd = 3, col = blue, ylim = range(Y[1, ], synth, colMeans(Y[-1, ])),
     xlab = "Month", ylab = "Resolutions per hour")
lines(1:TT, synth, lwd = 3, lty = 2, col = blue)
lines(1:TT, colMeans(Y[-1, ]), lwd = 2, lty = 3, col = grey)
abline(v = T0 + 0.5, col = orange, lty = 2)
legend("topleft", c("Manila", "Synthetic Manila", "Average of other offices"),
       col = c(blue, blue, grey), lty = c(1, 2, 3), lwd = c(3, 3, 2), bty = "n")

Design 6: Synthetic Control (continued)

How the Method Fits the Story

  • Simple comparison: difference-in-differences against the average of the other offices: 0.59. Too high, because Manila was growing faster than the average office.
  • Synthetic control: build a “synthetic Manila”: a weighted mix of offices chosen to match Manila’s path in months 1–12. After month 13, the gap between Manila and its synthetic version is the effect: 0.30.
  • Relies on: the mix that tracked Manila before month 13 continuing to track it.

Design 7: Regression and Matching

Managers give the tool mostly to experienced agents, and experience raises output. We observe each agent’s tenure.

Code 
set.seed(17)
tenure <- sample(1:4, n, replace = TRUE)                  # years on the job (observed)
tool_t <- rbinom(n, 1, c(0.15, 0.35, 0.65, 0.85)[tenure]) # more experienced, more likely to get it
y_t <- 1.6 + 0.2 * tenure + tau * tool_t + rnorm(n, 0, 0.3)
naive_t <- mean(y_t[tool_t == 1]) - mean(y_t[tool_t == 0])
reg_t <- unname(coef(lm(y_t ~ tool_t + factor(tenure)))["tool_t"])
gaps <- sapply(1:4, function(k) mean(y_t[tool_t == 1 & tenure == k]) - mean(y_t[tool_t == 0 & tenure == k]))
match_t <- sum(gaps * tabulate(tenure[tool_t == 1], 4)) / sum(tool_t)   # exact matching on tenure

mt <- tapply(y_t, list(tool_t, tenure), mean)
par(mar = c(4, 4, 1, 1))
matplot(1:4, t(mt), type = "b", pch = 19, lty = 1, lwd = 3, col = c(grey, blue), xaxt = "n",
        xlab = "Years of tenure", ylab = "Resolutions per hour")
axis(1, at = 1:4)
legend("topleft", c("No tool", "Tool"), col = c(grey, blue), lwd = 3, pch = 19, bty = "n")

Design 7: Regression and Matching (continued)

How the Method Fits the Story

  • Simple comparison: agents with the tool against agents without it: 0.53. Too high, because experienced agents resolve more anyway.
  • Regression and matching: compare agents with the same tenure (the gap within each tenure group in the plot), then average: 0.29 by regression, 0.28 by matching.
  • Relies on: tenure being the only thing managers used. If they also used something unobserved, such as skill in Design 1, this would fail.

legend("topleft", c("Simple comparison", "Design-based estimate", "Truth"),
       pch = c(1, 19, NA), lty = c(NA, NA, 2), col = c(grey, blue, orange), bty = "n")

Why the Estimates Agree Here

The tool has the same effect for every agent, so every design recovers 0.3. With real data, effects differ across people, and each design recovers the effect for a different group: everyone (experiment), agents who use the tool because of the email (IV), agents near the cutoff (RDD), or the offices and agents that were treated (DiD, synthetic control). The chapters return to this point.

LLMs as Research Tools

Measuring Variables with LLMs

LLMs are also increasingly used to measure variables, such as classifying text or rating job tasks. A measured variable is only as good as its validation: measurement error in a treatment variable biases estimates (Chapter 8 shows how fixed effects make this worse).

Six Questions for Any Evaluation

Use These for Any Evaluation You Read or Design

  1. Question: What is the causal effect of what, on what, for whom?
  2. Estimand: Is the target an ATE, an effect on the treated, or a local effect (compliers, units at a cutoff)?
  3. Identification: What comparison identifies the effect, and what assumption makes it valid?
  4. Threats: What would break that assumption in this setting?
  5. Evidence: How do the authors test or defend the assumption?
  6. External validity: Would the result hold in another place, time, or population?

Practicalities

Ethics in Policy Evaluation

Evaluations Involve Real People

  • Informed consent and institutional review: ethics board approval, and registration in a public trial registry
  • Equipoise: randomizing is easier to justify when we genuinely do not know whether the policy helps, or when resources are limited and must be rationed anyway
  • Privacy: linking survey, tax, health, and death records requires secure facilities and disclosure review
  • Distribution: average effects can hide harm to specific groups; check heterogeneity
  • Complete reporting: pre-registration, pre-analysis plans, and reporting null results

Summary

  • Policy evaluation asks what would have happened without the policy
  • Individual causal effects are never observed; we estimate averages using a comparison group
  • Naive comparisons mix the causal effect with selection bias, which can even flip the sign
  • Each method in these notes finds the counterfactual in a different place: chance, an outside shock, a cutoff, time, a synthetic unit, or observed characteristics
  • Credible evaluation is about design: a clear comparison, an explicit assumption, and evidence for it
