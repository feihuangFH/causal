Difference-in-Differences

Comparing Changes Over Time

Instructor

Fei Huang, UNSW Sydney

  • Difference-in-differences (DiD) is the most widely used design for evaluating policies that switch on for some units and not others.
  • It builds directly on Chapter 8: DiD is a two-way fixed effects model with a binary treatment that turns on at a known date.
  • We cover the 2×2 design, event studies, what goes wrong with staggered adoption, and inference. Then three case studies: Medicaid expansion and mortality, and two papers on generative AI at work.

Where This Chapter Fits

NoteStep 4: Do you observe the same units before and after treatment?
  • You arrive here when: A policy switches on for some units and not others, and you observe both groups before and after.
  • This chapter: Parallel trends, event studies, and what goes wrong with staggered adoption.
  • Next: Chapter 10: when only one or a few units are treated and no single comparison group is credible.

See the design flowchart in Chapter 1.

In Practice: Difference-in-Differences

TipPractitioner Summary
  • Use it when: a policy switches on for some units at a known date, and you observe treated and untreated units before and after
  • What you need: outcomes for both groups over several periods before and after the policy
  • What must hold: parallel trends without the policy; no anticipation; no spillovers onto the control group
  • Checks to run: a plot of raw trends; an event study and the power of its pre-trend test; a heterogeneity-robust estimator if timing is staggered; placebo groups and dates; clustering at the policy level
  • In R: feols(y ~ sunab(cohort, period) | unit + period, data), did::att_gt()
  • Telling a manager: “Before the policy the two groups moved together; afterwards the treated group pulled away by X. That gap is our estimate.”

Workflow: Difference-in-Differences

NoteSeven Decisions, in Order
Step Decision How to decide
1. Groups and timing Treated, control, when treatment starts One date or staggered?
2. Control group Never treated or not yet treated Why is its change a credible counterfactual?
3. Scale Levels or logs Decide before looking: parallel trends depends on the scale
4. Estimate 2×2, two-way fixed effects, or a robust estimator Staggered timing with changing effects → Callaway–Sant’Anna or Sun–Abraham
5. Event study Pre-trends and dynamics Report the power of the pre-trend test; run a sensitivity analysis
6. Inference Clustering level The level of the policy; wild bootstrap if clusters are few
7. Check and interpret Placebo groups and dates, spillovers; the ATT Scale by take-up if not everyone treated is affected

Each step is covered later in this chapter.

Motivation

Did Medicaid Expansion Save Lives?

NoteThe Policy Question

From 2014, the Affordable Care Act (ACA) let US states expand Medicaid to low-income adults. Some states expanded; others did not.

Question: Did the expansion reduce mortality among low-income adults?

WarningTwo Tempting but Wrong Comparisons

Before vs. after, in expansion states only: mortality changes over time for many reasons: people age, medicine improves, the economy moves. A before–after difference mixes the policy with all of these.

Expansion vs. non-expansion states, after 2014 only: the states that chose to expand differ systematically (income, politics, health systems). A cross-sectional difference mixes the policy with these differences.

The DiD Idea

TipUse Each Comparison to Fix the Other’s Problem
  • The control group’s change over time tells us how the treated group would have changed without the policy
  • Subtracting it removes common time shocks; differencing within groups removes fixed group differences
Before After Change
Treated (expansion states) \bar Y_{T,0} \bar Y_{T,1} \bar Y_{T,1} - \bar Y_{T,0}
Control (non-expansion states) \bar Y_{C,0} \bar Y_{C,1} \bar Y_{C,1} - \bar Y_{C,0}
Difference \hat\tau_{DiD} = (\bar Y_{T,1} - \bar Y_{T,0}) - (\bar Y_{C,1} - \bar Y_{C,0})
  • Classic example: Card and Krueger (1994), minimum wage in New Jersey vs. Pennsylvania fast-food restaurants.
  • Ask students: what would make the control group’s change a bad guess for the treated group’s counterfactual change?

The 2×2 Design

Setup and Target Parameter

NoteNotation
  • Units i in a treated group (D_i = 1) or control group (D_i = 0)
  • Two periods: before (t = 0) and after (t = 1); treatment switches on between them for the treated group only
  • Potential outcomes Y_{it}(1) and Y_{it}(0); observed Y_{it} = Y_{it}(0) + D_i \cdot \mathbb{1}[t = 1] \cdot (Y_{it}(1) - Y_{it}(0))

Target: the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT)

\tau_{ATT} = E[Y_{i1}(1) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = 1]

The problem: E[Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = 1], the treated group’s outcome without the policy after it started, is never observed.

The Key Assumption: Parallel Trends

ImportantParallel Trends

In the absence of treatment, the treated and control groups would have experienced the same average change in outcomes:

E[Y_{i1}(0) - Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i = 1] = E[Y_{i1}(0) - Y_{i0}(0) \mid D_i = 0]

Plus two supporting assumptions:

  • No anticipation: treatment does not affect outcomes before it starts, so Y_{i0} = Y_{i0}(0) for the treated group
  • No interference (SUTVA): the control group is not affected by the treated group’s treatment
TipIdentification in One Line

Under these assumptions, E[Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = 1] = E[Y_{i0} \mid D_i = 1] + \big(E[Y_{i1} \mid D_i = 0] - E[Y_{i0} \mid D_i = 0]\big), so

\tau_{ATT} = \big(E[Y_{i1} \mid D_i=1] - E[Y_{i0} \mid D_i=1]\big) - \big(E[Y_{i1} \mid D_i=0] - E[Y_{i0} \mid D_i=0]\big)

What Parallel Trends Does and Does Not Allow

NoteAllowed
  • Treated and control groups can have different levels of the outcome
  • Selection into treatment can depend on fixed characteristics, observed or not (the same idea as unit fixed effects in Chapter 8)
  • Common shocks that hit both groups equally
WarningNot Allowed
  • Selection on trends: units adopt the policy because their outcomes were already changing (e.g., the “Ashenfelter dip”: workers join training programs after an earnings drop, then recover anyway)
  • Group-specific shocks at the same time as the policy (another reform, a local recession)
  • Spillovers onto the control group
ImportantParallel Trends Depends on the Scale

Parallel trends in levels and in logs are different assumptions: if it holds for one, it generally fails for the other. It holds for every transformation of the outcome only under strong conditions, roughly that the whole distribution of untreated outcomes evolves in parallel, as it would under random assignment (Roth and Sant’Anna 2023). Choose the scale on substantive grounds before looking at results.

Regression Form

NoteThe 2×2 as a Regression

Y_{it} = \alpha + \beta D_i + \gamma \,\text{Post}_t + \tau \,(D_i \times \text{Post}_t) + \varepsilon_{it}

  • \beta: fixed difference between groups
  • \gamma: common change over time
  • \tau: the DiD estimate, numerically identical to the difference of the four means
TipWith Panel Data: Two-Way Fixed Effects

Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \tau \,\text{Treat}_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}

where \text{Treat}_{it} = D_i \times \text{Post}_t. Unit fixed effects \alpha_i replace \beta D_i; time fixed effects \lambda_t replace \gamma \,\text{Post}_t. This is the two-way fixed effects (TWFE) model from Chapter 8, with a binary treatment that turns on at a known date.

Covariates can be added, but only those not affected by treatment (otherwise they are bad controls).

A 2×2 DiD by Hand and by Regression

200 units are observed before and after a policy; half are treated. Treated units start at a higher level, which parallel trends allows, and everyone’s outcome rises by 0.5 over time. The true effect is 1.

Predict first: will the regression give the same number as the four means?

Code 
library(fixest)
setFixest_notes(FALSE)
set.seed(2026)

n <- 200
d22 <- expand.grid(id = 1:n, post = 0:1)
d22$treated <- as.integer(d22$id <= n / 2)

# Treated units start at a higher level (allowed under parallel trends)
unit_level <- rnorm(n) + 0.8 * (1:n <= n / 2)
d22$y <- unit_level[d22$id] + 0.5 * d22$post +          # common trend
         1.0 * d22$treated * d22$post +                  # true effect = 1
         rnorm(nrow(d22), 0, 0.5)

# Four means and their difference
means <- with(d22, tapply(y, list(treated = treated, post = post), mean))
round(means, 2)
       post
treated    0    1
      0 0.08 0.70
      1 0.75 2.27

A 2×2 DiD by Hand and by Regression (continued)

TipReading the Result

Treated units change by 2.27 − 0.75 = 1.52; controls by 0.70 − 0.08 = 0.62. The difference, 0.91, computed before rounding, is the DiD estimate (true effect 1). The interaction term in the regression gives the same number, with a standard error (0.10).

Event Studies

From One Coefficient to Many

NoteEvent-Study Specification

Let E_i be the period unit i is first treated (never-treated units have no event time). Estimate

Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \sum_{k \neq -1} \beta_k \,\mathbb{1}[t - E_i = k] + \varepsilon_{it}

  • \beta_k for k \geq 0 (lags): how the effect evolves k periods after treatment
  • \beta_k for k < -1 (leads): differences before treatment, which should be close to zero under parallel trends and no anticipation
  • k = -1 is the reference period, normalized to zero
  • Distant leads and lags are usually binned into end-point indicators
  • Every case study in this chapter shows an event-study plot. Make sure students can read one: flat pre-period, then the dynamic path of the effect.

Pre-Trend Tests: Handle with Care

WarningFlat Leads Are Evidence, Not Proof
  • Pre-trends test parallel trends before treatment; the assumption is about the counterfactual after treatment, which is untestable
  • Pre-trend tests often have low power: a violation large enough to matter may not be statistically detectable
  • Pretest bias: if we only report designs that pass a pre-trend test, the surviving estimates can be more biased, not less (Roth 2022)
TipBetter Practice
  • Report the power of the pre-trend test: how big a trend could you detect?
  • Use sensitivity analysis: how large could post-treatment violations of parallel trends be, relative to observed pre-trends, before the conclusion changes? (Rambachan and Roth 2023)
  • Show robustness to unit-specific or group-specific linear trends

Case study 1 (Miller et al. 2021) does all three.

Staggered Adoption

When Units Are Treated at Different Times

NoteStaggered (Rolling) Adoption

Most real policies roll out unevenly:

  • Medicaid expansion: 21 states in 2014, 3 in 2015, 2 in 2016, 1 in 2017
  • A firm gives an AI tool to different workers in different months
  • Laws adopted by different states in different years

The TWFE regression Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \tau \,\text{Treat}_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} is still the default. But with staggered timing and effects that vary over time or across cohorts, \hat\tau_{TWFE} can be badly misleading.

Why TWFE Breaks: The Goodman-Bacon Decomposition

WarningTWFE Is a Weighted Average of Many 2×2 Comparisons (Goodman-Bacon 2021)

With staggered timing, \hat\tau_{TWFE} averages every possible 2×2 DiD:

  1. Treated vs. never-treated units: fine
  2. Early-treated vs. not-yet-treated units: fine
  3. Late-treated vs. already-treated units, used as the “control”: the forbidden comparison

In comparison 3, the control group’s outcomes include its own treatment effect. If effects grow over time, the already-treated controls are trending up for reasons unrelated to the late cohort, and the comparison subtracts part of the true effect.

Result: the decomposition weights on the 2×2 comparisons are all positive, but because already-treated units serve as controls, some group-by-period treatment effects enter \hat\tau_{TWFE} with negative weights. \hat\tau_{TWFE} can then be far from any meaningful average effect, and in extreme cases have the wrong sign (Chaisemartin and D’Haultfœuille 2020).

Heterogeneity-Robust Estimators

TipThe Fix: Only Make Clean Comparisons
Estimator Idea R package
Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021) Estimate each cohort × period ATT against never- or not-yet-treated units, then aggregate did
Sun and Abraham (2021) Interaction-weighted event study: interact event time with cohort fixest::sunab()
Chaisemartin and D’Haultfœuille (2020) Compare switchers with units whose treatment did not change DIDmultiplegt
Borusyak et al. (2024) Impute Y(0) from untreated observations, then average didimputation
Arkhangelsky et al. (2021) Synthetic DiD: reweight units and periods to match pre-trends synthdid

Practical advice: with staggered timing, report a robust estimator alongside TWFE. If they agree, the TWFE weighting problem did little harm here. If they differ, the robust estimate is the better summary, but it still rests on parallel trends for its comparison group and on no anticipation. All three case studies in this chapter follow this advice.

Staggered Adoption: TWFE vs. a Robust Estimator

300 units: three groups adopt in periods 6, 11, and 16, and some never adopt. The effect grows the longer a unit has been treated.

Predict first: will TWFE be above or below the true effect?

Code 
# Three adoption cohorts plus never-treated units; effects grow over time
n_units <- 300
cohorts <- sample(c(6, 11, 16, Inf), n_units, replace = TRUE, prob = c(.3, .3, .2, .2))
stag <- expand.grid(id = 1:n_units, t = 1:20)
stag$first_treat <- cohorts[stag$id]
stag$event_time  <- stag$t - stag$first_treat
stag$treated_now <- as.integer(stag$event_time >= 0)
stag$tau <- ifelse(stag$treated_now == 1, 0.3 * (stag$event_time + 1), 0)
stag$y   <- rnorm(n_units)[stag$id] + 0.1 * stag$t + stag$tau + rnorm(nrow(stag), 0, 0.5)

# Static TWFE (uses forbidden comparisons)
m_twfe <- feols(y ~ treated_now | id + t, data = stag, cluster = ~id)

# Sun & Abraham (2021): never-treated cohort coded as a large number
stag$cohort <- ifelse(is.infinite(stag$first_treat), 10000, stag$first_treat)
m_sa <- feols(y ~ sunab(cohort, t) | id + t, data = stag, cluster = ~id)

data.frame(
  estimator = c("True ATT", "TWFE", "Sun & Abraham"),
  estimate  = round(c(mean(stag$tau[stag$treated_now == 1]),
                      coef(m_twfe)["treated_now"],
                      summary(m_sa, agg = "ATT")$coeftable["ATT", "Estimate"]), 2)
)
      estimator estimate
1      True ATT     1.97
2          TWFE     0.75
3 Sun & Abraham     2.01
TipReading the Result

The true average effect on the treated is 1.97. Static TWFE gives 0.75, well under half, because already-treated units act as controls for later adopters while their own effects are still growing. Sun & Abraham, which compares only with never-treated units, gives 2.01. With a constant effect, the two would agree.

Where the Static Bias Comes From

Code 
# TWFE event study: never-treated units get a reference event time
stag$rel <- ifelse(is.infinite(stag$first_treat), -1000, stag$event_time)
m_es_twfe <- feols(y ~ i(rel, ref = c(-1, -1000)) | id + t, data = stag, cluster = ~id)

iplot(list(m_es_twfe, m_sa), xlim = c(-8, 10), ylim = c(-1, 5),
      main = "Event studies: TWFE vs. Sun & Abraham",
      xlab = "Periods since treatment")
lines(0:10, 0.3 * (0:10 + 1), lty = 2, col = "grey40")
legend("topleft", c("TWFE", "Sun & Abraham", "True effect"),
       col = c("black", "#CC0000", "grey40"), lty = c(1, 1, 2), pch = c(20, 20, NA), bty = "n")
NoteReading the Plot

Both event studies track the true dynamic effect here, because each period since treatment gets its own coefficient, every cohort follows the same dynamic path, and never-treated units give clean comparisons. The problem is the single static coefficient, which averages these growing effects using already-treated units as controls. A TWFE event study is not safe in general: when the dynamic path differs across cohorts, or when periods are binned or dropped, each coefficient can mix in effects from other periods, and spurious pre-trends can appear (Sun and Abraham 2021).

Inference

Standard Errors in DiD

ImportantCluster at the Level of the Policy
  • Outcomes are serially correlated within units, and the treatment indicator is extremely persistent (0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1). Ignoring this badly understates standard errors (Bertrand et al. 2004)
  • Cluster at the level at which treatment is assigned (e.g., state for a state law)
  • With few clusters, or few treated clusters, cluster-robust standard errors are unreliable: use the wild cluster bootstrap or randomization inference (Chapter 8)

Real Data

Real Data: Minimum Wages and Teen Employment

Data that ship with the did package: log teen employment in 500 US counties, 2003–2007, a subset of the data in Callaway & Sant’Anna (2021). Treated counties are in states that raised the minimum wage in 2004, 2006, or 2007; controls stayed at the federal minimum.

Predict first: will TWFE and Callaway & Sant’Anna agree here?

Code 
suppressMessages(library(did))
data(mpdta)   # 500 US counties, 2003-2007; some states raised their minimum wage in 2004, 2006, or 2007
mpdta$treated_now <- as.integer(mpdta$first.treat > 0 & mpdta$year >= mpdta$first.treat)

# Static TWFE vs. Callaway & Sant'Anna (2021), which only compares with not-yet- or never-treated counties
twfe <- feols(lemp ~ treated_now | countyreal + year, data = mpdta, cluster = ~countyreal)
set.seed(2026)
cs <- att_gt(yname = "lemp", tname = "year", idname = "countyreal", gname = "first.treat", data = mpdta)

round(c(TWFE = unname(coef(twfe)), Callaway_SantAnna = aggte(cs, type = "simple")$overall.att), 3)
             TWFE Callaway_SantAnna 
           -0.037            -0.040
TipReading the Result

TWFE gives −0.037 and Callaway & Sant’Anna −0.040, about 4% lower teen employment. They are close here, so the TWFE weighting problem does little harm. That is a finding for this dataset, not a guarantee: always check.

Predict first: do you expect the effect to grow or fade after the increase?

es <- aggte(cs, type = "dynamic")
par(mar = c(4, 4, 1, 1))

The effect grows with time since the increase. Before the increase, the estimates are close to zero but not perfectly flat, which is when the power of the pre-trend test and a sensitivity analysis (Rambachan and Roth 2023) matter.

Case Study 1: Medicaid and Mortality

NoteMiller, Johnson & Wherry (2021), QJE (Miller et al. 2021)

Question: Did the ACA Medicaid expansions reduce mortality among low-income adults?

Data: a linkage of three federal sources:

  • American Community Survey (2008–2013) to identify people likely to gain eligibility: income at or below 138% of the poverty line or less than high school education; citizens; not already on disability benefits
  • Medicaid enrollment records to measure take-up
  • Census Numident death records to measure mortality through 2017

Sample: ~566,000 adults aged 55–64 in 2014 (a high-mortality group), excluding states that expanded before 2014.

Why it matters: earlier studies used aggregate death records and could not tell who was eligible, so they were underpowered to detect effects of plausible size.

Case Study 1: Specification

TipEvent Study with Staggered State Adoption

Y_{isjt} = \text{Expansion}_s \times \sum_{\substack{y=-6 \\ y \neq -1}}^{3} \beta_y \,\mathbb{1}[t - t^*_s = y] + \beta_t + \beta_s + \beta_j + \gamma\,\mathbb{1}[j = t] + \varepsilon_{isjt}

  • Y: died during year t (linear probability model); individual i, state s, survey wave j
  • \beta_s state FE, \beta_t year FE, \beta_j survey-wave FE
  • t^*_s: the state’s expansion year (21 states in 2014; 6 later)
  • \beta_y for y < -1: pre-trend checks; y \geq 0: effects in each year after expansion
  • Standard errors clustered by state; ACS survey weights

A subtle point: the sample is a fixed cohort that ages, so mortality rises over time everywhere. The pre-period mean is the wrong baseline; the authors compute a counterfactual post-period mortality rate instead.

Case Study 1: Checking the Design

WarningChecks the Authors Ran

First stage: Medicaid eligibility rises 49–51 pp and enrollment rises 12.8 pp in expansion states; flat beforehand.

Pre-trends: mortality trends are parallel before 2014. The authors then ask whether the test had power (Roth 2022): they could detect a linear pre-trend of 0.032 pp per year with 80% power; even such a trend would bias the estimate for event year 3 (the fourth year of expansion) by at most 0.089 pp, under half the estimated effect (0.208 pp).

Staggered timing: Goodman-Bacon (2021) decomposition shows only 11% of the TWFE weight comes from comparisons between differently timed states; dropping late expanders and using Sun & Abraham (2021) give similar results.

Placebo groups: no effect for people 65+ (already on Medicare), and only small effects for higher-income people, who were less likely to gain coverage.

Placebo period: no relative change in the pre-ACA period.

Other confounders: results are stable when adding state-specific linear trends, county economic trends, or alternative comparison groups.

Case Study 1: Results

TipMain Findings
  • Mortality falls by 0.132 percentage points per year, a 9.4% reduction relative to the sample mean
  • The effect grows over time: 0.089 pp in year 1, 0.208 pp by year 4
  • Driven by disease-related and health care–amenable deaths, not external causes such as accidents
  • Scaling: if non-expansion states had expanded, roughly 15,600 deaths could have been averted
NoteFrom Intent-to-Treat to the Effect of Coverage

The DiD estimate is an intent-to-treat effect of living in an expansion state. Dividing by the first stage gives the effect of Medicaid itself, the same logic as IV in Chapters 5–6:

\frac{\text{reduced form}}{\text{first stage}} = \frac{0.132 \text{ pp}}{0.375 \text{ years of Medicaid}} \approx 0.35 \text{ pp lower mortality per year of coverage}

This scaled effect is a LATE for compliers: people who enrolled because of the expansion.

Case Study 1: What to Copy

TipPractices to Reuse
  • Define the exposed group precisely. Linked records identify people likely to gain eligibility, instead of diluting the effect over everyone.
  • Report the power of the pre-trend test: the trend they could have missed would bias the estimate by at most 0.089 pp.
  • Use a group that should not respond (people aged 65+, already on Medicare) as a placebo, and scale the ITT by the first stage.

Case Study 1: Discussion Questions

  1. States chose whether to expand. Why doesn’t this choice by itself invalidate the design? What kind of state-level choice would?
  2. Why is the elderly (65+) sample a useful placebo? What would a significant effect in that group have suggested?
  3. The pre-trend test “passes”. Why do the authors still compute the size of trend they could have missed?
  4. Only 11% of the TWFE weight comes from timing comparisons. Why does that make staggered-adoption bias a small concern here, but potentially a large one in other settings?
  5. Why is the scaled effect (0.35 pp per year of coverage) a LATE, and for whom?
NoteSuggested Answers (click to expand)
  1. DiD allows expanding states to differ in levels, for example in income or politics. The design fails only if the expansion decision was related to trends in mortality, or coincided with other changes affecting low-income adults’ health.
  2. People aged 65 and over already have Medicare, so the expansion should not affect them. An effect there would point to state-level health shocks or policies that coincided with expansion, not Medicaid.
  3. A test with low power can pass even when the trend is large enough to matter. Computing the trend they could have missed (and the bias it would cause) shows whether passing the test is informative.
  4. Most of the weight comes from comparisons with states that never expanded, because few states expanded late. Where most units adopt at different dates and few are never treated, comparisons with already-treated units get more weight, and growing effects can bias TWFE badly.
  5. It divides the effect of living in an expansion state by the increase in Medicaid coverage. Under exclusion and monotonicity it is the effect per year of coverage for people who enrolled because of the expansion.

Case Study 2: Generative AI at Work

NoteBrynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (2025), QJE (Brynjolfsson et al. 2025)

Question: Does a generative AI assistant raise the productivity of customer-support workers, and who benefits most?

Setting: a Fortune 500 software firm’s customer support. An AI tool built on GPT-3 reads live chats and suggests responses; agents can accept, edit, or ignore suggestions.

Data: 3 million chats by 5,172 agents (89% outside the US, mainly the Philippines), aggregated to agent × month.

Main outcome: resolutions per hour (issues successfully resolved per hour).

Why DiD: the tool was not given to everyone at once. Access was rolled out agent by agent between fall 2020 and early 2021.

Case Study 2: Where the Variation Comes From

WarningRollout Was Not Random. Is It Good Enough?

Constraints that created staggered timing:

  • Agents had to complete a 3-hour training session, run by only two trainers with limited weekly capacity
  • The firm had a fixed contractual budget for AI seats
  • Managers spread a team’s members across different sessions to avoid service disruption

The worry: managers chose who to onboard. Ever-treated agents were already better before AI access (2.0 vs. 1.7 resolutions per hour).

Why the design can still work: agent fixed effects absorb level differences. The threat is selection on trends: managers picking agents who were about to improve anyway.

Case Study 2: Specification and Checks

TipTWFE with Agent and Time Fixed Effects

y_{it} = \delta_t + \alpha_i + \beta \,AI_{it} + \gamma X_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}

  • \alpha_i agent FE; \delta_t year-month FE; X_{it} tenure FE (months on the job, since agents improve with experience)
  • AI_{it} = 1 once agent i has access; standard errors clustered by agent

Robustness:

  • Event study with the Sun & Abraham (2021) estimator: flat before access, immediate jump after
  • Similar results with Callaway & Sant’Anna (2021), de Chaisemartin & D’Haultfœuille (2020), and Borusyak, Jaravel & Spiess (2024) estimators
  • IV: instrument each agent’s adoption date with the date the first person in their team got access (rules out within-team selection by managers)
  • A small earlier RCT pilot (~50 workers) gives similar effects

Case Study 2: Results

TipMain Findings
Specification (resolutions per hour) Estimate Fixed effects
Time + location FE +0.47 (+24%) Year-month, location
+ Agent FE +0.37 + agent
+ Tenure FE (preferred) +0.30 (+15%) + months of tenure

Adding agent FE shrinks the estimate: part of the raw gap was selection of stronger agents, which is what unit FE are for (Chapter 8).

  • Who gains: least-skilled and newest agents improve ~30%; the most skilled gain little, with a small decline in quality
  • Learning: agents with two months of tenure and AI perform like agents with six or more months without it; productivity stays higher even during AI outages
  • Work experience: customers are more polite and less likely to ask for a manager; attrition of new agents falls

Case Study 2: What to Copy

TipPractices to Reuse
  • Document how timing arose (trainer capacity, seat budgets) to argue it is unrelated to workers’ trends.
  • Report several heterogeneity-robust estimators next to TWFE.
  • Add a different design for the same question: a team-level instrument and a small randomized pilot.

Case Study 2: Discussion Questions

  1. Ever-treated agents were more productive before AI. Why is this a problem for a cross-sectional comparison but not, by itself, for DiD with agent FE?
  2. Why include tenure fixed effects in addition to agent and month FE? What bias would remain without them?
  3. Why does instrumenting with the team’s first adoption date address manager selection? What does it assume?
  4. The paper studies one firm in the medium run. What does it not tell us about generative AI’s effect on employment or wages?
  5. The most-skilled agents follow AI suggestions more over time, even though quality falls slightly. Connect this to the idea of “cognitive surrender” (Chapter 4).
NoteSuggested Answers (click to expand)
  1. A cross-section compares strong agents with weaker ones, which credits AI with pre-existing differences. With agent fixed effects each agent is compared with themselves, so level differences cancel; only differences in trends would bias the result.
  2. New agents improve quickly with experience, and agents gain access at different points in their careers. Without tenure effects, normal learning on the job would be counted as an AI effect.
  3. The team’s first access date depends on scheduling and team-level rollout, not on a manager picking particular agents within the team. It assumes team timing is unrelated to team trends and affects agents only through their own access, which spillovers within teams would violate.
  4. It says nothing about hiring, wages, or the number of support jobs, how other firms respond, or effects in other occupations and over longer periods.
  5. Accepting more AI suggestions while quality slips is consistent with adopting outputs with less scrutiny. But field data cannot separate over-reliance from a sensible choice to save time; the lab design in Chapter 4 can.

Case Study 3: Who Is AI Replacing?

NoteDemirci, Hannane & Zhu (2025), Management Science (Demirci et al. 2025)

Question: How did ChatGPT (and image-generating AI) change demand for freelance work, and for which kinds of jobs?

Data: all job posts on a leading global freelancing platform, 2021–mid-2023: 1.2 million fixed-price posts from 542,000 employers in 61 countries.

Treated and control groups: job posts are clustered by skill tags into categories, then grouped by exposure to language models:

  • Treated (automation-prone): writing; software, app and web development; engineering
  • Control (manual-intensive): data and office management, video services, audio services
  • A separate analysis treats graphic design and 3D modeling as exposed to image-generating AI (Midjourney, DALL-E 2, Stable Diffusion)

Case Study 3: Specification

TipDiD with a Single, Common Shock

y_{ctl} = \beta \,(\text{Post}_t \times T_c) + \gamma_{cl} + \gamma_t + \varepsilon_{ctl}

  • y: log number of job posts in category c, week t, country l
  • \text{Post}_t: after ChatGPT’s release (30 November 2022); T_c: automation-prone category
  • \gamma_{cl} category × country FE; \gamma_t week FE (seasonality, platform trends)
  • Event-study version by month to check pre-trends
  • Standard errors clustered by job category

Note: everyone is “treated” on the same date, so there is no staggered-adoption problem. The authors still report Callaway & Sant’Anna (2021) and synthetic DiD estimates, plus a negative binomial model because log counts with zeros are problematic (Chen and Roth 2024).

Case Study 3: Results and Checks

TipMain Findings
  • Job posts in automation-prone categories fall 21% relative to manual-intensive ones within eight months (coefficient −0.234 on log posts)
  • By category: writing −30%, software/app/web development −21%, engineering −10%
  • Image-generating AI: graphic design and 3D modeling posts fall 17%
  • The effect grows over time and after more capable model releases
  • Remaining automation-prone jobs pay more (+5.7% maximum budget), are more complex (+2.2% skill tags), and attract more bids (+8.6%)
NoteStrengthening the Causal Story
  • Dose-response: categories with larger increases in Google searches for “ChatGPT + [category]” saw larger declines
  • Placebo release dates give no effect
  • Robust to alternative control groups and to aggregating to category × week

Case Study 3: What to Copy

TipPractices to Reuse
  • When a shock hits everyone at once, compare exposed with unexposed categories, and argue that the controls are not exposed.
  • Add dose-response evidence (search intensity by category) and placebo release dates.
  • Pick a model that suits counts with zeros and be open about having few clusters.

Case Study 3: Discussion Questions

  1. The “treatment” is a worldwide technology release on one date. Why is a comparison across job categories still needed? What would a simple before–after comparison of writing jobs pick up?
  2. Could ChatGPT also affect the control categories (e.g., freelancers switching from writing to video editing, or clients using ChatGPT to write video scripts)? Which direction would that bias \hat\beta?
  3. Standard errors are clustered by job category, with only a handful of categories. What inference concern does that raise, and what would you do about it?
  4. Why might log(job posts) be a problematic outcome, and why does a negative binomial model help?
  5. Compare with Case Study 2: one paper finds AI raises worker productivity, the other finds AI reduces demand for freelancers. Can both be true?
NoteSuggested Answers (click to expand)
  1. Freelance demand changes for other reasons at the same time: seasonality, platform growth, interest rates, and tech-sector layoffs in 2022–23. A before–after comparison of writing jobs would attribute all of these to ChatGPT.
  2. If ChatGPT also reduces demand for control jobs, the control group falls too and the estimated decline is understated. If employers move budgets towards control categories, the control rises and the decline is overstated.
  3. With few clusters, cluster-robust standard errors are too small and tests reject too often. Use the wild cluster bootstrap or randomization inference across categories, and report results at several aggregation levels.
  4. The log of zero is undefined, and log(y + 1) gives results that depend on the units of y. A count model such as the negative binomial includes zeros and estimates a proportional effect directly.
  5. Yes. They study different margins and people: inside a firm, AI helps workers who use it, especially new ones; across a market, firms can do some outsourced tasks themselves, which cuts demand for freelancers. Higher productivity can also mean fewer hours are needed.

Best Practices

DiD Checklist

TipBefore Estimating
  1. Plot the raw data: treated and control group means over time
  2. Justify the control group: why is its change a credible counterfactual?
  3. Ask who chose treatment and why: selection on levels is fine; selection on trends is not
  4. Choose the outcome scale (levels vs. logs) before looking at results
  5. Decide the clustering level from how treatment was assigned
TipAfter Estimating
  1. Show an event study and discuss the power of pre-trend tests
  2. With staggered timing, report a heterogeneity-robust estimator alongside TWFE
  3. Run placebo tests: unaffected groups, fake treatment dates
  4. Check for spillovers onto the control group
  5. Interpret the estimand: ATT for whom, over what horizon; scale by the first stage if treatment take-up is incomplete

Common Pitfalls

WarningMistakes to Avoid
  • Treating flat pre-trends as proof of parallel trends
  • Running static TWFE with staggered adoption and dynamic effects
  • Using already-treated units as controls without realizing it
  • Clustering too finely (by individual when the policy varies by state)
  • Controlling for post-treatment variables that the policy itself changes
  • Ignoring anticipation: units changing behaviour before the official start date
  • Ignoring spillovers: the control group is partly treated
  • Taking logs of outcomes with many zeros without thinking about what the estimate means

Summary

Key Ideas:

  • DiD compares changes, not levels: the control group’s change stands in for the treated group’s counterfactual change
  • Identification rests on parallel trends, plus no anticipation and no spillovers
  • DiD is the two-way fixed effects model from Chapter 8 with a policy that switches on at a known date

Summary: Modern Practice and Case Studies

Modern Practice:

  • Event studies show dynamics and pre-trends, but pre-trend tests have limited power
  • With staggered adoption, TWFE can mix in forbidden comparisons; use robust estimators
  • Cluster at the policy level; beware of few clusters

From the Case Studies:

  • Linked administrative data plus careful placebo tests make the Medicaid–mortality result credible
  • Generative AI raises productivity most for less experienced workers inside a firm…
  • …while reducing demand for automation-prone freelance work across a platform
